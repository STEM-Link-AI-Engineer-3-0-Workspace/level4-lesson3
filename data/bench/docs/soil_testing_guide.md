# Soil Testing for Farmers — Taking a Sample and Reading the Report

Teaching corpus. Figures are illustrative and are not official recommendations.

## 1. Why test the soil

A soil test tells you which nutrients your field already has, so that you do not pay for fertilizer the crop cannot use. It also shows problems that fertilizer cannot fix, such as very acid or very alkaline soil.

## 2. Taking a sample

Take samples after harvest and before land preparation. Walk the field in a zig-zag and take 10 to 15 small samples from the top 15 cm of soil with a clean spade or auger. Avoid bunds, ash heaps and places where fertilizer was recently stored. Mix all the small samples in a clean bucket and send about half a kilogram to the laboratory in a labelled bag.

## 3. Reading the pH

Soil pH between 5.5 and 7.0 suits most crops. Below 5.0, the soil is strongly acid and iron and aluminium can reach toxic levels; apply dolomite to raise the pH. Above 7.5, the soil is alkaline, and zinc and iron become less available to plants even when they are present.

## 4. Reading the nutrient levels

The report gives available phosphorus in ppm. Below 10 ppm is low and the full phosphorus recommendation is needed; 10 to 20 ppm is medium; above 20 ppm is high and the phosphorus dose can be reduced. Exchangeable potassium is reported as low, adequate or high.

For zinc, a level below 1.0 ppm is considered deficient for paddy. Deficient fields should receive zinc sulphate before or at planting.

## 5. Electrical conductivity

Electrical conductivity measures salinity. Readings above 4 dS/m indicate a saline soil in which most paddy varieties suffer. Flush the field with fresh water and use a salt-tolerant variety.

## 6. How often to test

Test every three to four years, or sooner if yields fall without an obvious reason. Keep each report with the farm record so that changes over time can be seen.
