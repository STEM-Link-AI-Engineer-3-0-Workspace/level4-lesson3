# Paddy Pests and Diseases — Recognition and Control

Teaching corpus. Figures and products are illustrative and are not official recommendations.

## 1. Rice blast

Rice blast is caused by a fungus and can attack leaves, the collar of the leaf, nodes and the neck of the panicle. On leaves it produces spindle or diamond-shaped lesions with grey centres and brown margins. When the neck is infected the panicle breaks over and the grains stay empty, a condition farmers call neck rot.

Blast is favoured by cool nights, long dew periods and heavy nitrogen. Avoid excess urea, use a resistant variety where available, and spray tricyclazole 75 WP at 0.6 g per litre of water when leaf lesions are first seen. A second spray at the booting stage protects the neck.

## 2. Bacterial leaf blight

Bacterial leaf blight begins at the leaf tips and edges. The affected area turns yellow, then orange-yellow, and the lesion spreads down the leaf with a wavy margin. In the morning, milky droplets of bacterial ooze may be seen on young lesions. Severely infected fields look scorched from a distance.

There is no effective chemical cure. Drain the field for a few days, stop nitrogen top dressing until new leaves are healthy, and remove volunteer plants from bunds. Grow resistant varieties in fields with a history of the disease.

## 3. Sheath blight

Sheath blight produces oval, greenish-grey lesions on the leaf sheath near the water line. The lesions enlarge and join, and the tissue between them dries to a straw colour with dark borders. It spreads fastest in dense crops with high nitrogen and standing water. Reduce plant density, avoid excess nitrogen, and spray hexaconazole 5 SC at 2 ml per litre if more than 10 percent of tillers are affected.

## 4. Brown planthopper

The brown planthopper (BPH) is a small brown insect that sucks sap at the base of the plant, just above the water. Light infestations go unnoticed. Heavy infestations cause hopperburn: circular patches of plants that turn yellow, then brown, and collapse, usually starting in the middle of the field where the canopy is densest.

Tap the base of a few hills over a white tray. If more than 10 to 15 hoppers fall per hill, act. Drain the field for three to four days, which BPH dislikes. Avoid pyrethroid sprays, which kill the natural enemies of BPH and cause resurgence. Where spraying is needed, use buprofezin 25 SC at 1.5 ml per litre directed at the base of the plants.

## 5. Paddy bug

The paddy bug sucks the milk from developing grains, leaving them chaffy or with brown spots. It is active at the milky stage and smells strongly when disturbed. Keep bunds free of grass weeds where the bug shelters between seasons. Spray only when there is more than one bug per two hills at the milky stage.

## 6. Leaf folder

The leaf folder caterpillar folds a leaf lengthwise and feeds inside, leaving white streaks. Damage before panicle initiation is usually compensated by the plant. Spraying is rarely justified unless more than half the flag leaves are damaged.

## 7. Thresholds summary

The thresholds in this guide are economic thresholds: the pest level at which control costs less than the yield it saves. Spraying below threshold costs money, kills natural enemies and makes the next outbreak worse.
